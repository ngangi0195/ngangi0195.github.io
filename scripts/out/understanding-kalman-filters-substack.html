Understanding Kalman Filters

breaking down how Kalman filters work intuitively and the genius behind them through my perspective

It seems like Kalman Filters have a wide variety of applications – the general quality of being able to minimize uncertainty when making estimates has its benefits. This can include things like weather prediction, financial market analysis (quant), and most importantly, robotics optimization. In this post, I want to dive into how this algorithm works, its nuances, genius, and capabilities, all in a way that I understand it.

The Background

First off, the Kalman Filter actually describes an algorithm used for estimating and predicting the state of a system in the presence of uncertainty, such as measurement noise or other external factors.

This is where the Kalman Filter comes in. When I think of filters, I think of air filters that let clean air through and capture dirt. In a traditional "filter", what gets let through is discrete and the threshold of particle size that is allowed through is constant. Now, unlike an air filter, the Kalman Filter dynamically determines how much we should value new information at each step when determining the next step. Let's use our air filter example: this would be similar to being able to toggle how strong I want my filter to be; if I have a filter that can actively read incoming air quality and it seems to be good, then I won't filter as much, but if the air quality is terrible, then I can increase the strength of my filter based on the information I received. If our air filter was powered, a dynamic system that changes its output based on new information optimizes the way we use power – this dynamism is what makes the Kalman Filter a great algorithm.

Before continuing, we have to clarify what a measurement is; it is not a "one and done" where whatever we measure is ground truth, but is rather a single draw of a random variable that has some uncertainty, where the draw is the expected value and the uncertainty is the standard deviation.

In static contexts, this could cause your data to constantly jump around (measuring temperature in a room, for example), and in dynamic contexts, could cause a large amount of drift from your desired and actual outcome.

Here is a silly example: Say you buy a shirt for $117.84 and eat a 541-calorie meal, but shrug and log it as "eh, spent $100 and ate 500 calories." If your goal is to save money and get fit, these small discrepancies between your rough measurements and actual truth accumulate. Over time, that compounding error drifts your expected outcome away from reality—leaving you broke and out of shape instead of rich and healthy.

Note: The Core Idea

At every time step, the Kalman Filter blends two things: what your model predicts the state should be, and what your sensor actually measured. How much weight each gets depends on their relative uncertainties. This is the whole algorithm.

The Technical(ish)

Here, I will not go deeply into the derivations and linear algebra behind the calculations, but rather explain the intuition behind each calculation and how it plays into the algorithm. I highly suggest going to KalmanFilter.net to get the full depth and understanding of the algorithm, as this is where I learned for myself.

Think of a perfect lifeguard robot that aims to monitor the sea level of a local beach to protect the families and children looking to make lifelong memories from harm. It does this by pinging a buoy in the water for its reading for sea level + rate of change and uses it to see if there are any inconsistencies with the expected trend.

System: is local body of water

State: is the true sea level height (hhh) and rate of change (vvv) we are trying to accurately estimate

Our goal is to predict the next state given the current state all while integrating the real world inconsistencies. However, to predict the next state generally, we need a model that describes the pattern of how our state behaves. This is called our dynamic model, modeling out the best-guess for how our estimates change over time. In our case, the sea level rises and falls throughout the day, and we can model it as follows:

So, the genius of the Kalman Filter (and my favorite part) is utilizing Kalman Gain. At every time step, the Kalman Filter blends two things: what your model predicts the state should be, and what your sensor actually measured. How much weight each gets depends on their relative uncertainties in order to minimize the uncertainty of the estimated state. This can be expressed as the following:

x^1,1=K1z1+(1−K1)x^1,0\hat{x}_{1,1} = K_1 z_1 + (1 - K_1)\hat{x}_{1,0}x^1,1​=K1​z1​+(1−K1​)x^1,0​

As you can see, the first expression combines both forms of estimation with some weight KKK, where KKK is calculated by figuring the proportion of how significant our estimated uncertainty is compared to our total uncertainty, where the inverse handles the matrix division to solve for KKK. The Conclusion

There are many many many different applications for the Kalman Filter in the robotics context. Everything from estimating movement of joints to traversing across terrain to perception to swarm robotics. There also exists Extended Kalman Filters, which allow for non-linear state transition functions and use Jacobians of your non-linear function, so we would get Pn,n−1=JPn−1,n−1JT+QP_{n,n-1} = JP_{n-1,n-1}J^T + QPn,n−1​=JPn−1,n−1​JT+Q instead of Pn,n−1=FPn−1,n−1FT+QP_{n,n-1} = FP_{n-1,n-1}F^T + QPn,n−1​=FPn−1,n−1​FT+Q. Personally, I find it super cool and satisfying that this form of algorithmic learning doesn't require a massive dataset or training — it takes a dynamic approach instead. I'd love to try and apply this in a personal project and see how it works in the near future.

Glossary

"new information.": The difference between measured and predicted values.

covariance matrices: Represents the accumulation of uncertainty² for each variable across the diagonal, with uncertainty dependence on the off-diagonal (0 = independent).

covariance matrix: Represents the accumulation of uncertainty² for each variable across the diagonal, with uncertainty dependence on the off-diagonal (0 = independent).

dynamic model: A mathematical description of how the system's state evolves over time — the best-guess rule for how estimates change from one step to the next.

estimate: The best guess of the system's state, formed by considering both a measurement and a prediction based on their respective uncertainties.

estimating: Considering both a measurement and a prediction based on their respective uncertainties to make the best guess about the system's state.

extrapolation: Using the dynamic model and previous state + covariance estimates to predict the next ones.

measurement: A single draw from a random variable — not ground truth, but an observation with inherent uncertainty. The draw is the expected value and the uncertainty is the standard deviation.

measurement matrix: A matrix that transforms state information into measurements, putting them in the same scale/domain (ex: a digital thermometer monitors temperature as state, but measures electrical signal).

predicting: Making an educated guess of the system's state using a model (ex: guessing where a car will be in 10 seconds based on its current speed).

propagation: Taking initial data through a pass of a computational process to transform into another.

state: A vector of variables representing the true conditions/properties of a system at a given time (ex: [pos, vel]) that you want to estimate and predict with noise.

state transition matrix: Takes current values and updates them one step in time based on physical laws (ex: h=v⋅Δth = v \cdot \Delta th=v⋅Δt).

system: A subject that has attributes to be measured (ex: plane, car, ball).

variance: The square of the standard deviation. Used instead of standard deviation in computations to avoid sign issues when combining uncertainties.
