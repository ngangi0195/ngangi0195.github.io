Understanding Kalman Filters

July 13, 202623 min read

It seems like Kalman Filters have a wide variety of applications – the general quality of being able to minimize uncertainty when making estimates has its benefits. This can include things like weather prediction, financial market analysis (quant), and most importantly, robotics optimization. In this post, I want to dive into how this algorithm works, its nuances, genius, and capabilities, all in a way that I understand it.

The Background

First off, the Kalman Filter actually describes an algorithm used for estimatingConsidering both a measurement and a prediction based on their respective uncertainties to make the best guess about the system's state. and predictingMaking an educated guess of the system's state using a model (ex: guessing where a car will be in 10 seconds based on its current speed). the stateA vector of variables representing the true conditions/properties of a system at a given time (ex: [pos, vel]) that you want to estimate and predict with noise. of a systemA subject that has attributes to be measured (ex: plane, car, ball). in the presence of uncertainty, such as measurement noise or other external factors.

This is where the Kalman Filter comes in. When I think of filters, I think of air filters that let clean air through and capture dirt. In a traditional "filter", what gets let through is discrete and the threshold of particle size that is allowed through is constant. Now, unlike an air filter, the Kalman Filter dynamically determines how much we should value new information at each step when determining the next step. Let's use our air filter example: this would be similar to being able to toggle how strong I want my filter to be; if I have a filter that can actively read incoming air quality and it seems to be good, then I won't filter as much, but if the air quality is terrible, then I can increase the strength of my filter based on the information I received. If our air filter was powered, a dynamic system that changes its output based on new information optimizes the way we use power – this dynamism is what makes the Kalman Filter a great algorithm.

Before continuing, we have to clarify what a measurementA single draw from a random variable — not ground truth, but an observation with inherent uncertainty. The draw is the expected value and the uncertainty is the standard deviation. is; it is not a "one and done" where whatever we measure is ground truth, but is rather a single draw of a random variable that has some uncertainty, where the draw is the expected value and the uncertainty is the standard deviation.

In static contexts, this could cause your data to constantly jump around (measuring temperature in a room, for example), and in dynamic contexts, could cause a large amount of drift from your desired and actual outcome.

Here is a silly example: Say you buy a shirt for $117.84 and eat a 541-calorie meal, but shrug and log it as "eh, spent $100 and ate 500 calories." If your goal is to save money and get fit, these small discrepancies between your rough measurements and actual truth accumulate. Over time, that compounding error drifts your expected outcome away from reality—leaving you broke and out of shape instead of rich and healthy.

The Core Idea

At every time step, the Kalman Filter blends two things: what your model predicts the state should be, and what your sensor actually measured. How much weight each gets depends on their relative uncertainties. This is the whole algorithm.

There also exists Extended Kalman Filters, which allow for non-linear state transition functions and use Jacobians of your non-linear function, so we would get Pn,n−1=JPn−1,n−1JT+QP_{n,n-1} = JP_{n-1,n-1}J^T + QPn,n−1​=JPn−1,n−1​JT+Q instead of Pn,n−1=FPn−1,n−1FT+QP_{n,n-1} = FP_{n-1,n-1}F^T + QPn,n−1​=FPn−1,n−1​FT+Q.
